# The end-to-end run

Above the parity boundary, Python only. `backtest/` and `ingestion/history.py`.

Steps 1 through 8 each proved something in isolation. This is the first thing that runs all of
them against each other on a dataset with dates in it, and the first place the answer was
allowed to be **no**.

## A dataset with a signal planted in it

`spec/fixtures/datasets/synthetic_history` is generated by `ingestion/run.py --source
synthetic_history`. 120 business days from 2026-01-05, two underlyings, two expiries, five
strikes, calls and puts: 4800 rows over 240 partitions.

Two things about it are deliberate.

**The strike ladder is fixed at the first day's spot.** The first version re-rounded strikes
from each day's spot and produced 1068 distinct contracts, many quoted on a single day. Nothing
can be held for a reversion that takes a week if the contract stops existing tomorrow, and no
residual can be scored across time if there is no time. The ladder is fixed, so there are 40
contracts and every one of them is quoted on all 120 days.

**The signal is separated from the surface it hides in.** Level and skew are their own
mean-reverting factors, and on top of them a `richness` process multiplies the fair volatility
at named strikes only — two for `IDXH`, one for `NAMEH`. Its reversion is `0.12` a day, so it
has a lag-one autocorrelation of `0.88` and a half-life of about five days. `signal_truth.json`
records the fair and quoted volatility at every planted point, so the recovered signal can be
checked against the thing that was planted rather than against itself.

At amplitude `1.0` the planted richness runs from `-13.6%` to `+13.1%` of the fair volatility,
which on a 20% vol is `-2.7` to `+2.6` volatility points.

## Subtracting a mean is not subtracting a surface

The first strategy took the cross-sectional residual as a deviation from the average log
implied volatility. Nothing traded. The reason is worth keeping:

| | lag-one `rho` | effective `n` of 120 | naive z | adjusted z |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| deviation from the cross-sectional mean | `0.80` to `0.88` | `8` to `13` | `0.5` to `1.1` | `0.18` to `0.28` |

A deviation from the mean is mostly the contract's own fixed position on the skew, which does
not change from day to day, so it scores as persistent structure. Fitting the four named
factors of `factors.md` per observation date and taking the residual of *that* is the correct
subtraction, and it recovers the planted process: the residual's measured autocorrelation is
`0.848` against a planted `0.88`.

## The adjusted z-score is not an entry trigger

Even after the correct subtraction nothing traded, and this time the machinery was right and
the use of it was wrong.

```
naive    = (series[-1] - mean) / standard_deviation
adjusted = naive * sqrt((1 - rho) / (1 + rho))
```

The naive score asks **how dislocated is this contract now**. The adjustment is a discount for
persistence, and it is the right correction for a claim about a *mean*. Applied to a
current-value score and then compared against a fixed threshold it demands a naive z of `5.2`
before a `rho` of `0.85` residual can trade, which a stationary process will never produce.

Persistence does belong in the decision, but it belongs in **how much edge the trade is worth**,
not in whether the dislocation is real. It enters below as `1 - rho^h`.

## The measurement the whole program was for

Entry on the naive score at `1.5`, five lots, 40 days of warm-up, filled by crossing the spread
as `execution.md` requires:

| amplitude | underlying | gross | cost | net | contracts |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `1.0` | `IDXH` | `218,835` | `375,014` | **`-156,179`** | `1,400` |
| `1.0` | `NAMEH` | `11,075` | `78,509` | **`-67,434`** | `1,300` |

**The signal is found and the strategy still loses.** Gross is positive, repeatedly, on both
names: the pipeline recovers the planted richness. It is the spread that eats it.

### The cost is a fixed hurdle, and this is checkable

A z-score is scale-free, so raising the planted amplitude should leave the *same trades* and
scale only the gross. That is a prediction, and it holds:

| amplitude | `IDXH` gross | `IDXH` cost | contracts | `NAMEH` gross | `NAMEH` cost | contracts |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `1.0` | `218,835` | `375,014` | `1,400` | `11,075` | `78,509` | `1,300` |
| `1.5` | `303,948` | `374,118` | `1,400` | `15,137` | `78,468` | `1,300` |
| `2.0` | `388,274` | `373,252` | `1,400` | `19,183` | `78,429` | `1,300` |
| `3.0` | `585,663` | `372,319` | `1,400` | `27,214` | `78,353` | `1,300` |

The contract count is *identical* and the cost moves by `0.7%` across a threefold change in the
signal. So the break-even is a ratio and can be read straight off: `IDXH` crosses zero at an
amplitude of about **`1.84`**, and `NAMEH` needs about **`9.4`**.

**The same signal, at the same strength, is tradeable in the index and not in the single
name.** `NAMEH` quotes a 3% half-spread against the index's 1%, and carries one planted strike
against two. That is the whole difference, and no amount of better forecasting closes it.

### Trading less does not help

The obvious repair is to demand a bigger dislocation. It does not work:

| entry z | `IDXH` gross | contracts | gross per contract |
|---|---|---|---|
| `1.0` | `238,407` | `2,000` | `119` |
| `1.5` | `218,835` | `1,400` | `156` |
| `2.0` | `80,649` | `800` | `101` |
| `2.5` | `27,246` | `400` | `68` |

Gross per contract *peaks* at the threshold already in use and falls on both sides. The
problem is not churn, so tuning the threshold is not the answer — and tuning it on this data
would be the in-sample fit `research.md` and `hedging.md` both exist to forbid.

## Pricing the spread instead of thresholding

The answer is the one step 8 was built for: stop deciding with a threshold and decide with
`portfolio.md`, which already refuses a candidate whose edge is smaller than its spread.

Each contract becomes a candidate. Its expected edge is the modelled reversion, in currency:

```
capture   = -(current deviation) * (1 - rho^h)        h = 10 steps, two half-lives
d(vol)    = sigma * capture / 2                       w = sigma^2 T, so d log sigma = d log w / 2
edge      = vega * d(vol) * multiplier
spread    = 2 * half_spread * multiplier              in and out, both crossing
```

with the four factor loadings from the same per-date fit as the neutrality constraints, so the
book cannot express a level, term, skew or curvature view by accident. Persistence enters here,
through `1 - rho^h`, which is where it belonged.

| amplitude | underlying | rule | gross | cost | net | contracts | gross/contract |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `1.0` | `IDXH` | threshold | `218,835` | `375,014` | `-156,179` | `1,400` | `156` |
| `1.0` | `IDXH` | allocator | `232,124` | `187,026` | **`+45,098`** | `1,140` | `204` |
| `1.0` | `NAMEH` | threshold | `11,075` | `78,509` | `-67,434` | `1,300` | `8.5` |
| `1.0` | `NAMEH` | allocator | `2,086` | `3,272` | **`-1,186`** | `94` | `22.2` |
| `2.0` | `IDXH` | allocator | `487,343` | `265,817` | `+221,526` | `1,437` | `339` |
| `3.0` | `IDXH` | allocator | `581,964` | `265,249` | `+316,715` | `1,377` | `423` |

**Same data, same signal, same fills: a loss of `156,179` becomes a profit of `45,098`.** And
it is again not a trade-off along one axis — the allocator collects *more* gross while placing
*fewer* contracts, because `204` per contract against `156` means it is choosing better trades,
not merely fewer of them. This is the sequential-versus-joint result of `portfolio.md`
reappearing on real dates: the threshold rule was not at a worse point on the same frontier.

**And on `NAMEH` it declines.** Ninety-four contracts against thirteen hundred, and the loss
falls from `67,434` to `1,186`. Asked to trade a signal that cannot pay for its own spread, the
system very nearly refuses — which is the behaviour that matters, because refusing is the
answer a live book needs and a threshold rule cannot give.

It is not a rescue. `NAMEH` still loses at every amplitude tested, less than the threshold rule
does but never nothing. A rule that prices cost correctly turns an unprofitable signal into a
small loss, not a profit.

## The go/no-go

On this data, with these spreads: **`IDXH` yes above an amplitude of about `1.84` on a
threshold rule, and yes at `1.0` once the spread is priced. `NAMEH` no, at every amplitude
tested.**

The result to carry forward is not the profit. It is that the same signal is worth trading in
one instrument and not in another, that the difference is entirely the spread, and that the
component which discovered this is the allocator rather than the forecaster.

## What this run is not

Every number above is in sample, on synthetic data, against a signal whose generating process
is in this repository. It shows the pipeline can find a signal that is there and can decline
one it cannot pay for. It says nothing about whether such a signal exists in a real market, and
the registry, deflated Sharpe and PBO machinery of `research.md` is what a claim like that would
have to pass. Step 6's fill model is still uncalibrated against real fills, so the cost side —
the side that decided every result on this page — is the least trustworthy part of it.

## Invariants under test

- the per-date factor fit leaves residuals orthogonal to every factor, and summing to nothing
- the expected capture opposes the current deviation and is zero at the mean
- a contract with no history is not traded
- the threshold rule finds the planted signal and still loses money on it
- pricing the spread turns the same signal profitable, with more gross from fewer contracts
- the allocator declines the single name the threshold rule trades thirteen hundred times
